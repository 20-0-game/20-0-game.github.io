20-0 Running Back Draft Guide

Stewart leads rushing yards in this pair; Davis leads yards from scrimmage. Follow the receiving work before deciding which back, and which destination, serves your roster.

🏃 A backfield comparison needs two lanes

A running back can reach his production through carries, receptions, or a substantial share of both. In the 20-0 game, looking only at the rushing-yard column can hide an important part of the offered card. Looking only at total yards can hide how that production was assembled.

Ground work

Rushing yards, rushing touchdowns, and yards per carry describe the running side. Read the workload before turning a small efficiency difference into a decisive edge.

Receiving work

Receptions, receiving yards, and receiving touchdowns show the second route. They are part of the back’s contribution, not a separate receiver you get to draft alongside him.

The point is to describe the actual profile before choosing its destination. A runner’s real football usage and his eligibility for RB or FLEX are related questions, but they are not the same question.

Stewart gives the ground-focused side of the comparison

The Jonathan Stewart card captured here records 824 rushing yards and nine rushing touchdowns. Its receiving line adds eight receptions, 60 yards, and no receiving touchdowns. That is a very different distribution from a back who adds a large receiving workload.

The card’s 3.8 YPC is an average on rushing attempts. It does not mean every carry gained that amount, and it does not summarize the receiving work. A single per-carry number should not replace the rest of the profile.

Stewart is a useful example because his strengths are readable without turning him into a caricature. “Ground-focused in this captured version” is supported. “Never useful as a receiver in his career” would be a much broader claim.

📊 How much of the yardage came through the air?

Divide receiving yards by rushing plus receiving yards. This describes the share of the two visible yardage categories; it is not a game-rating formula or a measurement of receiving skill independent of opportunity.

That difference is large enough to change how you describe the cards. Davis has fewer rushing yards in this pair, but receiving production closes the gap and takes his combined yardage above Stewart’s.

Davis changes the story after the rushing column

The Mike Davis card shows 642 rushing yards, six rushing touchdowns, 59 receptions, 373 receiving yards, and two receiving touchdowns. His expanded view also displays 1,015 scrimmage yards, consistent with the two yardage components.

Use that profile to challenge a quick first impression. If you stopped at 824 versus 642 rushing yards, you would miss 313 additional receiving yards on Davis’s card. If you stopped at combined yards, you would miss Stewart’s touchdown advantage in the pair.

Stewart’s rushing lead is 182 yards; Davis’s receiving lead is 313. The receiving difference is larger, producing Davis’s 131-yard lead across the two categories. Stewart still has nine combined touchdowns to Davis’s eight. That is the tradeoff to explain before considering the slot.

A tiny average gap deserves a workload check

The displayed YPC values differ by 0.1 and are rounded. Dividing rushing yards by that rounded figure will not reliably recover the exact carry count. Read the workload if supplied; otherwise keep the rate comparison narrower than an invented denominator.

🔎

Before trusting an efficiency headline

Check whether the cards show comparable opportunity. When a useful denominator is absent, keep the conclusion narrow rather than inventing a carry count. A rate helps explain a workload; it does not make workload irrelevant.

For unfamiliar abbreviations, use the stat dictionary. For overall evaluation limits, the ratings guide explains why these card observations do not expose the full model.

🧩 Choose RB or FLEX with the next vacancy in mind

RB is still empty

A legal back can solve the fixed role directly. Placing him there preserves flexible destinations for later runners, receivers, or tight ends. Spending FLEX first needs a specific reason, not just a habit of clicking the nearest highlighted box.

RB is already occupied

Another back competes for the flexible room that remains. Compare him with the other eligible skill players in this pool and notice which fixed vacancy still needs an answer.

There is no published bonus here for collecting different running styles or pairing a runner with a favorite quarterback. Treat real football chemistry as a discussion topic, not a scoring rule you can assume.

Describe the back before you draft him

If Stewart first caught your eye at 824 rushing yards, reopen Davis before deciding. If Davis first caught your eye at 1,015 scrimmage yards, revisit the touchdown line. Each first impression misses something the other card makes visible.

When the back competes with a receiver for the final shared slot, move to the wide receiver guide for a receiving-specific comparison. The overall decision sequence then helps resolve the roster question around both cards.
